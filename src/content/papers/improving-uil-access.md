> **Discussion draft.** Newton Prep is not a UIL member, has not applied for membership, and has received no indication that UIL supports the concept described here.

## Summary

Should Texas permit a durable, accountable home-school organization to become a member of the University Interscholastic League, provided that organization can assume the substantive responsibilities of membership?

Newton Prep examines that question through its Robeson initiative, named in honor of Paul Robeson, the scholar, artist, and athlete. We favor broader access to academic, artistic, and athletic competition. This inquiry asks whether an organizational membership model can provide that access while maintaining competitive integrity, institutional accountability, and home-school independence.

We offer this paper as a starting point for discussion with UIL and other interested institutions. In particular, we seek UIL's perspective on three questions:

1. Does UIL believe it has authority, through its Legislative Council and the applicable approval process, to establish a membership category for qualifying home-school organizations?
2. What substantive legal, competitive, or operational obstacles would UIL identify?
3. What process and participants would be appropriate for examining the model's feasibility?

Texas has already made the fundamental policy decision that bona fide home-school students may participate in UIL activities while retaining their home-school status. Current law provides a route through public schools, subject to district opt-outs. UIL also permits a limited category of private schools to become members. These arrangements raise a straightforward question: can a sufficiently accountable private organization become a UIL member to represent bona fide home-school students?

UIL has legitimate interests in eligibility, recruiting and transfers, safety, enforcement, classification, scheduling, administrative capacity, and institutional continuity. Our guiding principle is **no special advantages, no special disabilities**: apply existing safeguards wherever they fit, identify appropriate equivalents where conventional-school mechanisms do not translate, and consider new rules only where a structural difference requires them.

Newton Prep has begun a practical experiment for the 2026–27 academic year: voluntarily applying relevant UIL standards to its programs wherever feasible. We call it the Shadow UIL Project and plan to continue it through 2027–28. Its purpose is to replace speculation with experience.

Robeson will document that work alongside legal and historical research, comparisons with other interscholastic organizations, unresolved questions, and reusable compliance materials. This paper outlines the research program. Our objective extends beyond Newton Prep: to determine whether a durable institutional pathway could give bona fide home-school students meaningful access to the full range of UIL competition.

## 1. The problem

The State of Texas has decided that home-school students should have access to UIL activities and has backed that decision with financial support. School districts are entitled to an annual allotment of $1,500 per participating non-enrolled student for each UIL activity in which the student participates, subject to the funding requirements. See Texas Education Code §48.305, implemented by [19 TAC §105.1031](https://tea.texas.gov/laws-and-rules/commissioner-rules-tac/coe-tac-currently-effect/ch105dd.pdf).

Yet even with this clear legislative intent and state financial support, many school districts have declined to accept non-enrolled students into their UIL activities.

UIL stands alone among Texas interscholastic leagues and conferences in the breadth and level of competition it offers. Its competitions span mathematics, science, computer science, robotics, writing, speech and debate, music, theatre, and numerous sports. Access to those opportunities matters across the full range of a student's education.

An obvious question arises: could a different approach fulfill the Legislature's intent that home-school students be able to participate in UIL activities while preserving school districts' choice to decline their participation?

## 2. Texas has already separated UIL representation from academic enrollment

Texas Education Code §33.0832 establishes a pathway through which qualifying non-enrolled students may participate in UIL activities through public schools. Following the 2025 amendments, a public school generally must provide qualifying non-enrolled students the same opportunity to participate unless its governing body elects not to do so. The statute carries residence, academic, eligibility, and other safeguards, and it preserves significant protections against governmental regulation of the underlying home school.

The conceptual importance of this statute is substantial. Historically, the ordinary model was simple: school educates student, student represents school in UIL. Texas law now permits a second model: home school educates student, public school represents student in UIL. The UIL member representing the student, in other words, need not be the institution providing that student's general elementary or secondary education. Any analysis of institutional home-school participation should start from that fact.

## 3. The "Jones test"

A thought experiment helps distinguish student eligibility from the institution responsible for representation. Assume Jones High School sits in a district that permits home-school participation, and a particular UIL team or contingent consists entirely of qualifying non-enrolled home-school students. Those students remain home-schooled while representing Jones High School.

Now consider the same students representing a durable home-school organization. Which responsibilities and safeguards would that organization need to assume before the arrangement could serve UIL's purposes as effectively?

Jones High School brings established governance, attendance boundaries, facilities, enforcement authority, and continuity. Newton would need to demonstrate how it could fulfill those institutional responsibilities.

Which features of a conventional member school are essential to UIL representation, and could an organization such as Newton Prep provide them or an appropriate equivalent?

## 4. Existing private-school membership is an important precedent

UIL membership already encompasses private institutions. Dallas Jesuit and Strake Jesuit participate in UIL following unusual historical circumstances surrounding the collapse of the Texas Christian Interscholastic League. UIL ultimately modified its rules to permit certain accredited private schools to apply, subject to restrictions that include placement in the largest conference and a requirement concerning eligibility for membership in another organization “similar to the UIL.”

Jesuit and Strake educate their students. A home-school member organization would represent students whose families retain responsibility for their education. Section 33.0832 already permits that separation of education and representation through public schools. The question is whether the two arrangements can responsibly be combined.

We seek UIL’s perspective on the authority, rules, and safeguards that such an arrangement would require.

## 5. The "similar to UIL" requirement requires much more study

One potentially important issue is UIL's existing requirement that a qualifying private school not also qualify for membership in another organization "similar to the UIL." The meaning of “similar” requires examination. Texas has numerous interscholastic organizations — the Texas Association of Private and Parochial Schools (TAPPS), the Southwest Preparatory Conference (SPC), the Texas Christian Athletic League (TCAL), the Texas Christian Athletic Fellowship (TCAF), the Texas Home School and Independent League (THIL), the Texas Association of Independent Athletic Organizations (TAIAO), and potentially others that belong in a comprehensive survey. The survey should examine how well each organization serves the students and activities at issue.

Consider the college analogy. The NCAA and NAIA both govern intercollegiate athletics, with materially different competitive opportunities. Comparing their functions alone would tell us little about the additional opportunities NCAA membership might offer a particular institution.

That distinction suggests at least three possible meanings of similarity: institutional similarity (does the organization structurally resemble UIL?), functional similarity (does it perform the same general function of organizing interscholastic competition?), and competitive substitutability (does it actually provide substantially comparable competitive opportunity?). The current rule’s meaning and the merits of a future rule are separate questions. Our policy comparison should examine membership size, geographic distribution, classifications, competitive depth, advancement structures, championships, governance, eligibility administration, enforcement, and the breadth of activities offered. Each organization should be assessed on its actual offerings and the students it serves.

We presently suspect that no other Texas organization approaches UIL in combined breadth, scale, competitive depth, geographic reach, and advancement opportunity. We intend to test that hypothesis empirically. We also need historical research into why UIL adopted the "similar organization" restriction, what purpose the Legislative Council intended it to serve, and how UIL has interpreted it since. Understanding that history and UIL’s interpretation will help us distinguish the requirements of the current rule from possible policy changes.

## 6. Legitimate UIL concerns

A workable membership model must address legitimate UIL concerns.

**Institutional durability.** UIL organizes competition on multiyear alignment cycles, and member institutions, opponents, administrators, and communities schedule and commit in reliance on other members continuing to exist. An informal team organized around one parent and a handful of students could disappear quickly and create genuine disruption. A home-school member organization should demonstrate sufficient institutional permanence and capacity to fulfill its obligations throughout the applicable alignment period — legal existence, independent governance, financial capacity, insurance, administrative systems, record retention, safeguarding procedures, and other evidence of continuity. Ordinary UIL members add and discontinue programs. A durability requirement should allow those changes while ensuring that the member organization can meet its continuing obligations.

**Recruiting and competitive aggregation.** UIL's existing safeguards governing athletic-motivated transfers, recruiting, residence, and prior participation should apply wherever they sensibly fit. A home-school membership model must provide effective controls against assembling elite competitors through recruiting or transfers that would be prohibited elsewhere.

**Academic eligibility.** Home schooling necessarily requires a different mechanism for establishing eligibility, since the member organization may not issue the student's grades. Texas law already addresses this problem for non-enrolled students, and those statutory mechanisms should be our starting point. We must also define what it means to be home-educated for purposes of participation, consistent with Texas law and clear enough for a member organization to administer.

**Institutional accountability.** UIL must have someone to hold responsible. A member organization must maintain responsible administrators, certify eligibility, preserve records, cooperate with investigations, administer contests, accept sanctions, and enforce UIL decisions. This may be the single most important distinction between a bona fide home-school member organization and an informal team.

**Student safety.** Physical examinations, concussion protocols, heat safety, abuse prevention, background screening, and other appropriate safeguards should protect students participating through home-school organizations. We must identify the requirements applicable to each activity and the means of carrying them out.

**Administrative burden.** The home-school member organization would administer participation and serve as the institutional point of contact with UIL, consolidating the responsibilities associated with its participating families.

## 7. "No special advantages. No special disabilities."

Our method begins with existing requirements. For each one, we ask, in order:

1. Can a home-school organization comply with the rule as written?
2. If not, does the statutory framework for non-enrolled students provide an appropriate mechanism?
3. Does UIL's treatment of existing private members provide a useful analogue?
4. If none of these fits, what equivalent safeguard or new rule would address the structural difference?

Any adaptation should preserve the requirement’s legitimate purpose. We should evaluate its administrative cost, its effect on access, and any competitive advantage it might create.

## 8. Preserving local control

Texas has established two policies that must coexist. The state has expanded access to UIL for home-school students, and at the same time local school districts retain authority to opt out of serving non-enrolled students. A home-school membership model could accommodate both policies.

A district may prefer to reserve its coaching, supervision, facilities, and administrative resources for its enrolled students. A home-school family may prefer to continue an educational arrangement that serves its child well. A home-school member organization could accommodate both: the district retains its opt-out, the family retains its home-school status, the organization assumes the institutional responsibilities, and UIL regulates competition.

## 9. The Shadow UIL Project

During 2026–28, Newton is voluntarily applying relevant UIL standards to its own operations wherever feasible. The Shadow UIL Project will document the practical demands of operating to those standards.

For each relevant requirement, we want to know: What does UIL require? What legitimate purpose does the requirement serve? Can Newton comply literally? If not, why not? Can Newton accomplish the same purpose through an appropriate alternative? What administrative burden does compliance impose? What does it cost? What institutional authority or infrastructure is missing? What did we learn from actually trying?

Over time we expect requirements to sort into categories: works as written; works with administrative adaptation; difficult but feasible; and cannot presently be implemented outside UIL membership or conventional-school infrastructure. The last category may prove the most valuable, because it identifies institutional gaps encountered in practice.

## 10. Early lessons from the shadow project

Even at this early stage, different kinds of requirements are becoming visible. Physical examinations translate relatively easily: Newton can use the standard UIL pre-participation physical form, adjust its operational calendar, set deadlines, and maintain appropriate records.

Background screening is a harder problem. Newton can decide, on its own authority, that adults with recurring, supervisory, transportation, overnight, or unsupervised access to students should receive appropriate screening regardless of whether they are employees, contractors, volunteers, or parents. But obtaining access to the same fingerprint-based Texas DPS/FBI infrastructure available to conventional public-school systems raises questions of statutory authorization and agency procedure. Our research has identified potentially relevant federal and Texas statutory pathways, but the precise administrative route remains unresolved — which is exactly the kind of problem the Shadow UIL Project is meant to uncover. Resolving this issue requires identifying the legal authority and administrative procedure through which Newton can obtain appropriate screening.

## 11. Preserving home-school independence

The proposal must preserve the family’s authority over its home school. Educational and competitive responsibilities should be clearly assigned.

A possible architecture: the family home school controls education, curriculum, credits, advancement, transcript, and graduation; the home-school member organization administers UIL representation, eligibility procedures, coaches and sponsors, competition, safety, and records, and bears institutional accountability; UIL regulates interscholastic competition. Students would retain their home-school status and their families would retain control of their general education.

This issue deserves particular scrutiny from home-schooling advocacy organizations, and we would welcome criticism of any proposed mechanism that could inadvertently weaken Texas home-school independence.

## 12. Other institutional models

We have also examined special-purpose school districts and a possible statutory hybrid. Texas law authorizes special-purpose school districts for students whose educational needs are not adequately met by regular districts. UT High School and Texas Tech University K-12 are examples of statewide or nontraditional special-purpose structures, and Boys Ranch ISD is especially instructive — a special-purpose public school district operated in connection with a private nonprofit institution serving a distinctive student population. Texas has, historically, been willing to build unusual institutional forms where ordinary school-district structures do not fit a student population.

A home-school special-purpose district nevertheless presents substantial complications. Existing special-purpose-district law generally assumes student enrollment and educational responsibility, which could conflict with preserving bona fide home-school status, and creating a public entity would introduce issues of funding, accountability, public records, governance, employment, procurement, student records, and other public-school laws.

We regard this model as worthy of study but presently less attractive than allowing an accountable private home-school organization to become a UIL member. A third possibility is a statutory hybrid: a legislatively recognized home-school interscholastic organization with defined authority and responsibilities for administering UIL participation. Its legal status would require careful consideration. We should explore the simpler membership approach with UIL and other stakeholders first.

## 13. Understanding the purposes of membership requirements

Membership requirements should be examined in light of the institutional purposes they serve. Some purposes—student safety, eligibility enforcement, reliable scheduling, and competitive integrity—are already central to this paper. Others may emerge from UIL's history, governance responsibilities, and experience administering competition.

We seek UIL's help in identifying those purposes and explaining where a home-school membership model would present difficulties. For example, understanding the purpose and application of the “similar organization” restriction would help determine whether it addresses concerns that a proposed category must also resolve.

This inquiry should distinguish three questions: what the current rules require, what authority exists to change them, and what policy would best serve students and member institutions. A proposed alternative must address all three.

## 14. Scope of the proposal

The proposed pathway would preserve district opt-outs and family control of home-school education. Member organizations would meet academic eligibility standards, comply with recruiting and transfer rules, accept UIL investigations and sanctions, and demonstrate the capacity to administer their programs. Classification would turn on competitive considerations. Each applicant would have to establish its qualifications for membership. This proposal seeks no public funding for Newton Prep or any other home-school organization.

The rule should be capable of serving ten responsible home-school organizations as readily as one.

## 15. What we may ultimately propose

The concept under examination is narrow: a bona fide organization representing bona fide home-school students should be eligible to apply for UIL membership if it can demonstrate the capacity, durability, accountability, competitive integrity, student protections, and administrative competence necessary to assume membership responsibilities. UIL would evaluate each application against those requirements.

Classification requires separate study. UIL already provides distinct classification rules for private members and charter schools. Those arrangements offer precedents for considering how home-school organizations might be classified. We should examine their application across activities and their effects on competitive balance and meaningful participation.

Any different treatment should respond to a demonstrated structural difference between a conventional school and a home-school member organization.

## 16. From white paper to public research project

This paper introduces the inquiry. The broader Robeson site will bring together the supporting work: primary sources and legal history; comparisons of interscholastic organizations; Shadow UIL Project findings; concerns and proposed safeguards; possible institutional models; and, when sufficiently developed, draft rule or statutory language. The site will distinguish completed research from planned work and unresolved questions.

Robeson also publishes a proposed Interscholastic Standard for review. As this inquiry develops, we will explain how that draft relates to the Shadow UIL Project and which provisions might inform a future membership proposal.

Periodic updates will summarize findings, identify revisions, and make clear which conclusions the evidence supports.

## 17. An open-source approach

Newton Prep has an institutional commitment to sharing its work so others can build upon and improve it, and that principle is particularly appropriate here. Where legally and practically appropriate, Newton can publish the procedures, checklists, forms, compliance calendars, eligibility processes, safety policies, administrative templates, and lessons developed during the Shadow UIL Project. If another home-school organization wishes to operate to comparable standards, it should not have to repeat every piece of work Newton has already done.

We should also publish mistakes and unsuccessful approaches. If we initially interpret a requirement incorrectly, discover that a procedure does not work, or identify an unexpected burden, those experiences are part of the research. The project should remain useful even if Newton Prep never becomes a UIL member.

## 18. Public engagement

We welcome scrutiny of the proposal. Concerns about its effects on students, schools, and competition should be identified and addressed as the work develops.

We would particularly welcome input from UIL administrators; TEA and Commissioner personnel; SBOE members and staff; public-school superintendents and administrators; athletic directors and coaches; academic, music, theatre, robotics, and speech and debate administrators; Texas home-school organizations and families; existing private-school associations; contest officials; attorneys familiar with Texas education law; student-safety professionals; and students themselves.

Public engagement should focus on evidence and specific questions. The project should make it easy to report factual errors, submit contrary authority, identify overlooked operational problems, and propose better solutions — and when criticism reveals an error, we should correct it publicly.

## 19. Proposed engagement with UIL

We would welcome an initial discussion with UIL about authority, substantive obstacles, and the appropriate process for examining feasibility. Our first institutional question is whether UIL believes it presently has authority, through its Legislative Council and the applicable approval process, to establish a membership category for qualifying home-school organizations.

We also seek guidance on the requirements such organizations would need to satisfy, the stakeholders who should participate in the inquiry, and the evidence UIL would find useful. Concerns or objections should help define the work to be done, including circumstances in which the model may prove impracticable.

If the inquiry identifies a useful model that requires additional statutory authority, narrowly tailored legislation could be considered as an enabling step. The immediate purpose is to understand the available authority and practical requirements before developing a formal proposal.

## 20. Potential legislative issues

If legislation eventually becomes necessary, it should be as narrow as reasonably possible. Among the matters that may require attention: authority for UIL to recognize home-school member organizations; preservation of bona fide home-school status; protection against regulation of the underlying home school; institutional eligibility and accountability; application of appropriate student-safety requirements; access to the necessary Texas DPS/FBI fingerprint-based background-check infrastructure; eligibility certification; interaction with §33.0832; district opt-out and local-control protections; and any governmental authority necessary for the organization to discharge responsibilities ordinarily performed by member schools.

A useful governing principle: if Texas expects a home-school member organization to assume the responsibilities of a UIL member school, Texas should provide the legal tools reasonably necessary to perform those responsibilities.

## 21. Questions we have not yet answered

This work is in its first stages. Among the important unresolved questions: What did UIL originally mean by an organization "similar to the UIL"? Why was that restriction adopted? How has UIL interpreted it since adoption? What Texas organizations currently qualify as plausible alternatives, and how do their actual competitive opportunities compare with UIL's? What UIL requirements cannot presently be implemented by a private home-school organization under existing law? Which requirements merely require administrative adaptation? What institutional durability standard would appropriately protect UIL alignments without discriminating against new but capable organizations? How should residence and transfer rules apply to an organization without conventional attendance boundaries? What is the precise DPS/FBI background-screening pathway available to an organization such as Newton? Does UIL believe existing law gives it authority to establish this membership category? Would the Commissioner of Education approve such a rule? What concerns would ISDs identify that we have not yet considered? What concerns would home-schooling advocacy organizations identify regarding preservation of home-school independence?

These are important research questions that deserve serious study.

## Conclusion

Texas provides a pathway for qualifying home-school students to participate in UIL through public schools while retaining their home-school status. This paper asks whether a durable, accountable organization formed to serve those students could also assume the responsibilities of UIL representation.

Newton Prep supports broader access. Whether this particular model can provide it fairly and practicably remains to be established through research, operational experience, and discussion with UIL and other affected institutions.

We invite UIL's perspective on its authority to consider the concept, the obstacles it identifies, and a suitable process for examining feasibility. That conversation would help turn the questions in this paper into a focused program of work.
